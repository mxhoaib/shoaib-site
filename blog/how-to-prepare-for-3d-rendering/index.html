Photorealistic renders help clients say "yes" faster. They make a design tangible before anything is built, and they reduce costly changes later. Yet many rendering projects lose days because the visualiser is missing key information. Whether you are an interior designer in London, a developer in Dubai or a kitchen company in Vancouver, here is how to prepare your project for 3D rendering services.

Decide what the render is for

A marketing image for a brochure is very different from a design-review render. Be clear about:

  • Purpose: client approval, marketing, planning submission or internal review.
  • Number of views and the rough camera positions.
  • Output size: social media, website hero, or large-format print.
  • Style: realistic daylight, evening mood, clean studio look.

Knowing the purpose lets the visualiser balance detail against time and cost.

Provide accurate drawings

Renders are built from geometry. The most useful inputs are:

  1. Floor plans with dimensions.
  2. Elevations for every wall that will be visible.
  3. Ceiling plans including lighting positions and ceiling heights.
  4. Sections for double-height spaces, stairs or complex joinery.
  5. Joinery and stone details for feature items like vanities or reception desks.

CAD files (DWG) are ideal because they can be imported directly. PDFs work but take longer to model.

Specify materials precisely

"Grey marble" can mean a hundred different stones. Wherever possible, share:

  • Manufacturer and product names for tiles, laminates and paints.
  • Photos of the actual stone slabs, especially for feature walls.
  • Finish details: polished, honed, matt, satin, brushed.
  • Wood species and stain colour.

For paint, giving a colour code from a recognised system helps the visualiser match it closely. Keep in mind that screens vary, so final colour approvals should still use physical samples.

Furniture, lighting and decor

Share links or model numbers for key furniture and light fittings. If exact models are not essential, mood images are enough for the visualiser to select similar pieces. Lighting is especially important: tell them the colour temperature (for example 3000K warm white) and whether pendant or linear lights should be visible.

Camera views and reference images

Mark camera positions on the plan with arrows. Add a few reference images showing the mood, composition or lighting you like. References are not for copying; they simply help align expectations.

The review process

A typical rendering workflow is:

  1. Clay or white model views to confirm geometry and camera angles.
  2. First draft with materials and lighting.
  3. Revisions based on your consolidated comments.
  4. Final high-resolution images and any post-production.

Approving the camera angles at the clay stage is the best way to avoid expensive late changes, because moving a camera after lighting and materials are set can mean re-rendering everything.

Common causes of delay

  • Missing ceiling heights or window sizes.
  • Changing the design after modelling has started.
  • Vague material descriptions.
  • Feedback from several people arriving separately.

Renders for stone and marble projects

Stone suppliers and fabricators benefit greatly from renders that show the actual slab pattern on a feature wall or island. Combining real slab photos with accurate shop-drawing geometry shows the client where veins will fall before any cutting. This is an area I specialise in because I also prepare marble shop drawings.

File formats you will receive

Final renders are usually delivered as high-resolution JPG or PNG files. For web use, ask for compressed versions as well; modern formats like WebP load faster, which helps your website performance.
