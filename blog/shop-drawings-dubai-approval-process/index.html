Shop Drawings in Dubai: Understanding the Submission and Approval Process

On almost every construction and fit-out project in Dubai and the wider UAE, subcontractors must submit shop drawings before they can fabricate or install. For a stone, joinery, ceiling or MEP subcontractor, getting these drawings approved quickly is often the difference between hitting a programme date and paying for delays. This article walks through how the process typically works and how to avoid the most common rejection reasons.

Who asks for shop drawings?

The chain usually looks like this: the client appoints a consultant (architect, interior designer or engineer) and a main contractor. The main contractor appoints subcontractors for specialist packages. Each subcontractor prepares shop drawings, which pass through the main contractor to the consultant for review.

On larger projects, there may also be a project management company and, for certain items, authority requirements. For example, fire-rated doors, façades and life-safety systems often involve approvals from the relevant authorities, such as Dubai Civil Defence for fire and life safety, and building permits through Dubai Municipality. Always check which approvals apply to your package.

What consultants look for

Consultants review shop drawings against the design drawings and specifications. They typically check:

  • Compliance with the approved design intent.
  • Correct materials, finishes and thicknesses as per the specification.
  • Coordination with other trades (MEP, ceilings, joinery, stone).
  • Practical fixing and support details.
  • Dimensions that match site conditions.

A submission that clearly shows these points is more likely to receive an "approved" or "approved with comments" status on the first attempt.

Typical submission package

A professional submission normally includes:

  1. Transmittal or submittal form in the main contractor's format.
  2. Drawing list with sheet numbers, titles and revisions.
  3. General arrangement plans referencing the design drawings.
  4. Elevations, sections and details.
  5. Material references linking to separately approved material submittals.
  6. Coordination notes with other trades where relevant.

Use the project title block and numbering system. Inconsistent numbering is a surprisingly common cause of administrative rejection.

Review status codes

While each consultant has their own system, many use a four-level code such as:

  • A: Approved, proceed.
  • B: Approved with comments, proceed incorporating comments.
  • C: Revise and resubmit, do not proceed.
  • D: Rejected.

Read the comments carefully. A "B" status still requires the comments to be incorporated, and many contractors resubmit for record.

Site measurement versus design dimensions

In fit-out work, design drawings are often prepared before walls are built. Shop drawings should be based on site measurements wherever possible, especially for stone, joinery and glass. Where site measurement is not yet possible, clearly note that dimensions are to be verified on site, and plan a final check before fabrication.

Coordination is everything

Many rejections come from clashes: a vanity that conflicts with a drain, a cladding panel cut through a socket, or a ceiling bulkhead that hits a sprinkler. Overlaying your drawings on the MEP and reflected ceiling plans before submission catches most of these problems early.

Tips for faster approvals

  • Cloud and tag every revision, and update the revision table.
  • Respond to every comment, even if just to confirm it was incorporated.
  • Keep drawing scales consistent and readable when printed at A3 or A1.
  • Include clear, scaled details for edges, joints and fixings.
  • Submit related items together so the consultant sees the full picture.

Working remotely on UAE projects

Not every subcontractor has a full-time drafting team. Remote drafters familiar with UAE practice can prepare submissions from site measurements, design PDFs and DWGs, then revise them against consultant comments. The key is a drafter who understands local conventions such as metric units, A1/A3 sheet sizes and typical consultant comment formats.
