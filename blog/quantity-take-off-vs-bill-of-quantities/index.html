Quantity Take-Off vs Bill of Quantities: What Is the Difference?

Estimators, contractors and suppliers use the terms quantity take-off and bill of quantities almost interchangeably, but they are not the same document. One is the measuring work; the other is the organised, priceable result. Knowing the difference helps you ask for the right deliverable, compare tenders fairly and avoid paying for work twice. This guide answers the questions people most often search for on the topic.

What is a quantity take-off?

A quantity take-off (often shortened to QTO or "take-off") is the process of measuring every item of work from the drawings and listing the quantities. The estimator reads plans, sections, elevations and specifications, then counts and measures: square metres of flooring, linear metres of skirting, cubic metres of concrete, the number of doors, and so on.

The output is usually a set of measurement sheets or a spreadsheet. Each line shows where the quantity came from, such as the drawing number, room or grid line, plus the dimensions used. That traceability matters: when a drawing changes, you can see exactly which quantities need updating.

What is a bill of quantities?

A bill of quantities (BOQ) is a structured document that presents the measured quantities in a standard order, with clear item descriptions, units and empty rate and amount columns for pricing. It is typically split into sections such as preliminaries, substructure, superstructure, finishes and external works, or by trade.

Because every bidder prices the same descriptions and quantities, a BOQ makes tenders directly comparable. After award it often becomes the basis for interim valuations and variations.

Quantity take-off vs bill of quantities: what is the real difference?

Think of it as process versus product:

  • Take-off is the measurement work and its working sheets.
  • BOQ is the formal, organised list built from those measurements, written to a measurement standard and ready for rates.

You can have a take-off without a BOQ. A subcontractor pricing only stone cladding, for example, might measure the areas and go straight to a quote. You cannot produce a reliable BOQ without a take-off behind it.

Which measurement standards are used for a BOQ?

The standard depends on the country and client. Commonly referenced ones include NRM2 in the UK, published by the Royal Institution of Chartered Surveyors, and the POMI (Principles of Measurement International) approach that is still seen on Middle East projects. In North America, owners often rely on CSI MasterFormat divisions to organise estimates rather than a formal BOQ. Always ask which standard the client expects before you start measuring, because units and rules differ, for instance whether small openings are deducted from wall areas.

How do you do a quantity take-off step by step?

  1. Collect the latest drawings and specs. Confirm revision numbers so you measure the current design.
  2. Set up a structure. Use trades or work sections, plus a consistent unit for each item (m², m, m³, nr).
  3. Measure systematically. Work room by room or grid by grid, in the same direction every time, so nothing is missed or counted twice.
  4. Record the source. Note the drawing and location against each quantity.
  5. Apply deductions and allowances consistently. Follow the agreed standard for openings and add waste factors only where the client wants them shown.
  6. Check. Compare totals against a quick rough estimate and look for obvious outliers.
  7. Issue. Deliver the measurement sheets, a summary and, if required, the BOQ.

Can a take-off be done from PDF or CAD drawings?

Yes. Most take-offs today are done digitally. With DWG files, areas and lengths can be measured accurately from the geometry itself. With PDFs, the drawing must be calibrated to a known dimension first. When a drawing is a scan or a sketch, it is often worth having it redrawn as clean CAD so the quantities can be measured precisely and re-measured after revisions.

How are finishes like marble and stone measured?

Finishes need more care than structural items because material is bought in slabs or tiles, not in square metres. A good stone take-off records:

  • the net finished area of each surface (floor, wall, vanity, step);
  • linear metres of edge work, such as polished, bullnose or mitred edges;
  • counts of cut-outs for basins, hobs and taps;
  • thickness and finish for every line.

From there, the net area is converted into slabs using real slab sizes and a waste allowance. I explain that conversion in detail in how to calculate marble quantity in square metres and square feet.

Who needs a quantity take-off or BOQ?

  • Contractors pricing tenders who need accurate quantities fast.
  • Developers and clients who want a BOQ so bids are comparable.
  • Fit-out and joinery firms pricing packages from design drawings.
  • Stone suppliers and fabricators checking how many slabs a project needs.
  • Architects and interior designers who want a budget check before the design is frozen.

What should a take-off deliverable include?

At minimum: a summary by trade, detailed measurement sheets with drawing references, a list of assumptions and exclusions, and the revision of drawings used. If a priced estimate is needed, rates should be clearly separated from quantities so they can be updated independently.
