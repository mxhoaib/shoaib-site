Ordering too little marble stops a job; ordering too much ties up money in stone you cannot return. Whether you are a fabricator, contractor or homeowner, the method for working out marble quantity is the same: measure the net area, add the right allowances and convert the result into the slabs or tiles you will actually buy. This guide walks through each step and answers the questions people commonly search for.

How do you calculate marble in square feet?

Multiply length by width for each surface, in feet, and add the results together.

  • A floor 12 ft by 10 ft is 120 sq ft.
  • A countertop 8 ft long and 2 ft deep is 16 sq ft.

If you measure in inches, multiply length by width in inches and divide by 144 to get square feet. For irregular rooms, split the plan into rectangles and triangles, calculate each one and add them up. Measure walls, floors, steps and vanity tops separately, because they often use different thicknesses or finishes.

How do you convert square feet to square metres for stone?

One square metre equals about 10.764 square feet. To convert:

  • square feet ÷ 10.764 = square metres
  • square metres × 10.764 = square feet

Suppliers in the UAE, UK and Canada usually quote in square metres, while US suppliers often use square feet. Keep one unit throughout your calculation and convert only at the end to avoid rounding errors.

How much waste should you allow for marble?

There is no single correct figure. Waste depends on the layout, the slab size and how fussy the pattern is. Typical allowances used in the trade:

  • simple straight-lay tile floors: a modest allowance for cuts and breakages;
  • diagonal or herringbone patterns: noticeably more, because of the extra cuts;
  • slab work with cut-outs, curves or many small pieces: more again;
  • book-matched or vein-matched feature walls: the highest, because pieces must come from consecutive slabs in the right position.

Ask your fabricator for their usual factor, and record it as a separate line so the client can see net area and waste clearly.

How many marble slabs do I need?

Work it out from real slab sizes, not just area. A slab's usable area is smaller than its nominal size because edges are often chipped or uneven and must be trimmed.

  1. Find the usable slab size from your supplier, for example the trimmed length and height.
  2. Lay out each piece on the slab, on paper or in CAD, keeping vein direction consistent where it matters.
  3. Count how many slabs the layout needs.

Dividing total area by slab area gives a minimum, but it almost always understates the real number because pieces do not tile perfectly into a slab. A drawn slab layout is the only reliable answer for countertops, vanities and feature walls.

What is the difference between net area and gross area?

Net area is the finished surface you see once installed. Gross area is net area plus waste, offcuts and trimming. Estimates should show both. Net area is what you charge the client for installation; gross area is what you buy from the supplier.

How do you measure marble edges and cut-outs?

Edges and cut-outs are priced separately from area, so measure them separately:

  • Edges: linear feet or metres of each profile, such as eased, bullnose, ogee or mitred.
  • Cut-outs: count basins, hobs, taps and sockets, and note whether undermount sinks need polished cut-outs.
  • Returns and upstands: measure as strips with their own height.

These items are where quantities are most often missed, so check them twice.

Does thickness change the quantity?

Thickness does not change the surface area, but it does change the slab you order and the cost. Floors and walls often use thinner material than countertops, and laminated or mitred edges can make a thin slab look thicker. List thickness against every line of your take-off so the supplier quotes the right material.

Should you use a marble quantity calculator?

Online calculators are fine for a quick budget figure. They multiply area by a waste factor. They cannot see vein direction, slab defects, book-matching or the real shapes of your pieces. For anything beyond a simple tiled floor, a drawn slab layout and cutting list gives a far more dependable number.

How do shop drawings help with marble estimation?

Shop drawings give each piece a unique ID, exact dimensions and an edge detail. Once the pieces exist as drawn shapes, they can be arranged on slab photos to count slabs, check veining and produce a cutting list. That is why many fabricators prepare marble and stone shop drawings before confirming the stone order. My complete guide to marble shop drawings covers what those drawings include.

Should you order extra marble for repairs?

Many fabricators keep a small spare piece from the same block for future repairs, because a later batch may not match in colour or veining. Discuss this with the client at ordering stage.
