Marble Shop Drawings: A Complete Guide for Fabricators and Contractors

Marble and natural stone are unforgiving materials. Once a slab is cut, there is no undo. That is why marble shop drawings sit at the centre of every successful stone package, whether it is a hotel lobby in Dubai, a kitchen in Toronto or a bathroom refurbishment in London. This guide explains what a good set of stone shop drawings contains, how the approval process usually works, and the mistakes that most often lead to wasted slabs.

What are marble shop drawings?

Shop drawings are the fabricator's interpretation of the architect's design intent. The architectural drawings show what the finished space should look like; the shop drawings show exactly how each stone piece will be cut, finished, fixed and installed. They are prepared after site measurement and are submitted to the consultant or main contractor for approval before fabrication begins.

For stone work, a complete package typically includes:

  • Layout plans showing every piece with a unique reference number.
  • Elevations for walls, cladding, vanities and staircases.
  • Sections and details showing edge profiles, fixing methods, joints and substrates.
  • Piece drawings (also called cutting drawings) with exact dimensions for the workshop.
  • A cutting list summarising piece IDs, sizes, thickness, finish and quantity.
  • Material schedule with stone name, finish (polished, honed, leathered), and thickness.

Start with accurate site measurements

Every stone drawing is only as good as the measurements behind it. Walls are rarely perfectly square, floors are rarely perfectly level, and openings move during construction. A practical approach is to draw the space from measured dimensions, check diagonals, and record any out-of-square conditions directly on the plan. Where tolerances matter, such as countertops between two walls, the drawing should state whether pieces will be scribed on site or cut to the measured size.

Joint layout and vein direction

Joint layout is where design and economics meet. Joints should be placed where they are least visible, aligned with features such as door frames or sink centres, and kept consistent in width. For veined stones like Calacatta or Statuario, the drawing should also indicate vein direction and any book-matched or slip-matched panels. Marking this clearly prevents a workshop from cutting a beautiful slab in the wrong orientation.

A useful habit is to overlay piece outlines on photos of the actual slabs before cutting. This visual allocation lets the client approve exactly which part of which slab ends up on the feature wall.

Edge profiles and details

Edge details are a frequent source of misunderstanding. Common profiles include:

  • Eased (small arris) edge
  • Half bullnose and full bullnose
  • Bevel or chamfer
  • Mitred edge with a built-up apron
  • Ogee and other decorative profiles

Each profile should appear as a scaled section in the shop drawings, with the build-up thickness and any laminated strips shown. For mitred aprons, specify the angle and whether the joint is reinforced.

Fixing methods and substrates

Consultants want to see how the stone will stay in place. For wall cladding, details should show the substrate, adhesive or mechanical anchors, and any movement joints. For countertops, show support brackets, cabinet tops and overhang limits. If you are unsure about the right system, refer to the manufacturer's technical data and to guidance from organisations such as the Natural Stone Institute.

Cutting lists and slab optimisation

A clear cutting list saves hours in the workshop. Each line should include the piece ID, drawing reference, length, width, thickness, finish, edge work and quantity. When combined with slab dimensions, it allows the fabricator to plan nesting, estimate the number of slabs required, and calculate waste percentage before ordering material.

Remember that the saw blade removes material too. Allow for kerf (blade width) and any trimming needed to remove slab edges or fissures.

The approval process

The usual cycle looks like this:

  1. Prepare drawings from site measurements and the architectural design.
  2. Submit to the main contractor and consultant.
  3. Receive comments, often coded A (approved), B (approved with comments) or C (revise and resubmit).
  4. Revise, cloud the changes, and update the revision table.
  5. Issue for fabrication once approved.

Keep a clean revision history. Clouded changes and a revision block in the title sheet make it obvious what changed and why.

Common mistakes that waste stone

  • Missing dimensions on piece drawings, forcing the workshop to guess.
  • Ignoring vein direction on feature walls.
  • Forgetting the thickness of the adhesive bed or substrate.
  • Not coordinating with MEP for sockets, sinks and taps.
  • Using inconsistent piece IDs between plans and cutting lists.

Working with a remote drafter

Many fabricators in the USA, UK, Canada and the UAE now outsource stone drafting. It works well when you share site measurements, architectural PDFs or DWGs, material details and the expected edge profiles from the start. A remote drafter can then return editable AutoCAD files, PDFs and cutting lists ready for submission.
