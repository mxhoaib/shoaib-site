"How much will it cost?" is the first question on every project, yet the answer can only be as good as the information behind it. A figure based on a sketch and a figure based on a full set of drawings are both estimates, but they deserve very different levels of confidence. Estimate classes give everyone a shared language for that confidence. This guide explains them and answers common questions.

What are construction cost estimate classes?

The most widely referenced classification comes from AACE International in its recommended practice 18R-97. It defines five classes, from Class 5 (the earliest and least defined) to Class 1 (the most detailed). The class depends mainly on how much of the project has been defined, not on how much effort the estimator spends.

What are the five estimate classes?

  • Class 5 – concept screening. Very little definition; based on comparable projects or cost per square metre.
  • Class 4 – feasibility or study. Early design; key systems are known and parametric or assembly costs are used.
  • Class 3 – budget authorisation. Design is developed enough to measure major quantities; often used to approve funding.
  • Class 2 – control or bid. Detailed design; most work is measured from drawings and priced with unit rates.
  • Class 1 – check estimate or bid. Near-complete design; full take-off and detailed pricing.

How accurate is each estimate class?

AACE gives ranges rather than single percentages, because accuracy depends on project type and complexity. In broad terms, a Class 5 estimate may be out by a large margin either way, while a Class 1 estimate is expected to fall within a much narrower band. The practical message is that early estimates should always be shown with a range and a contingency, never as one precise number.

What information does each class need?

Early classes rely on project size, type, location and historical costs. As you move towards Class 1 you need developed drawings, specifications, a quantity take-off, supplier quotations and a construction programme. The step that most often moves an estimate up a class is measuring real quantities instead of using cost per square metre.

How does a quantity take-off improve accuracy?

A take-off replaces assumptions with measured facts: actual areas of finishes, lengths of walls, counts of doors and fixtures. Each quantity can then be priced with a current rate. If you are unsure how a take-off differs from a bill of quantities, read quantity take-off vs bill of quantities.

Why do finishes cause estimate overruns?

Finishes such as marble, stone, joinery and specialist ceilings are often described loosely in early design ("marble flooring to lobby") and only fixed later. The chosen stone, slab size, pattern and edge details can change the cost dramatically. Measuring finishes properly, and converting stone area into real slab numbers, removes one of the biggest sources of surprise. My guide on calculating marble quantity shows how.

What is contingency and how much should be included?

Contingency is an allowance for costs that are expected but not yet defined. It is not a buffer for scope changes the client has not asked for. Contingency should shrink as the estimate class improves: generous at concept stage, smaller once a full take-off and quotes are in. Agree the contingency approach with the client and show it as a separate line.

How do you present an estimate clearly?

  • State the estimate class and the drawings and revision used.
  • Separate quantities, rates and totals.
  • List assumptions, exclusions and risks.
  • Show contingency and any escalation separately.
  • Give a range for early estimates, not a single figure.

When should you get a professional estimate?

Before committing to a budget, before tendering, and whenever the design changes significantly. Architects and interior designers often benefit from an independent check at concept and developed-design stages, and contractors need fast, accurate quantities at tender.

What is the difference between an estimate and a quotation?

An estimate is a calculated forecast of cost, with stated assumptions and a range of uncertainty. A quotation is a firm offer to do defined work for a stated price, usually valid for a limited time. Contractors build their quotations from estimates, but clients should not treat an early estimate as a price they can hold anyone to.

Which tools are used for construction cost estimating?

Estimators use a mix of spreadsheets, measurement software that works directly on PDF or CAD drawings, and BIM models from which quantities can be extracted. The tool matters less than the discipline: a clear structure, traceable measurements and rates that are current for the location. Clean, well-layered CAD drawings make measurement faster and more reliable, which is why messy scans are often redrawn in AutoCAD before a detailed take-off.

How often should an estimate be updated?

Update the estimate at every design stage and whenever there is a significant change to scope, specification or market prices. Keeping each version, with the drawings it was based on, lets you explain to the client exactly why the number moved.
